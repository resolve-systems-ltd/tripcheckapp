The copies that vanish when the cabin network never connects

Rick Steves tells travelers to photograph or scan the passport, the visa, and the key vouchers before departure, and to keep a digital copy on the phone as well as one with somebody at home. That advice meets a limit the moment the aircraft has no network. On the Rick Steves forum, travelers describe the same gap: a scan sitting in Google Drive is no help if the Wi-Fi never connects, so they also save a copy into the phone itself, or mark the file available offline. Lonely Planet makes the boarding-pass version of the point. A mobile pass is not a plan if the airport will not accept it, or if the battery dies, or if the data runs out. What remains is whatever you already stored on the device. TripCheck lists offline documents, so the vault you opened on that phone can still be read in airplane mode. The gate when email will not load

The attendant wants the passport page, the visa, or a confirmation number. You open the inbox and it spins. The photo you remember is in a chat on another phone. Lonely Planet's warning about a mobile boarding pass is the same failure in a smaller frame: the phone was supposed to be the document, and then it was not. A cloud folder assumes the connection you do not have. Rick Steves's backup only works in the cabin if the copy is already on the handset, not merely uploaded somewhere you cannot reach. Travelers on the forum say this plainly. Redundancy is useless if every copy needs the network that just disappeared.

Put the copies on the phone while you still have signal

The evening before you fly, open TripCheck on the phone you will carry and look at each document you might be asked to show. That visit is what leaves the offline copies in place.
